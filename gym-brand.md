# Gym Wear Brand — Launch Plan

A concrete, activewear-specific roadmap. Gym wear is *not* a generic clothing brand — it has
technical fabrics, fit-or-die quality standards, and a community-driven marketing model. This
plan is built around those realities.

> **The market:** athleisure is projected to hit **~$660B by 2030**, and the 2026 trend is
> *transitional* pieces — gym-to-street hoodies, joggers, sports bras, and leggings people
> wear all day. That's your opening: a brand with a point of view, not just workout gear.

---

## 1. The one decision that shapes everything: POD vs. manufacturing

There's a hard truth most "start a clothing brand" guides skip:

- **Print-on-demand (POD) works for:** tees, oversized gym shirts, hoodies, joggers,
  crewnecks — anything that's a **printed design on a blank garment.** Zero inventory, ~10–20%
  margin. Great for testing your brand and building an audience cheaply.
- **POD does NOT work for:** real technical **leggings, sports bras, seamless sets,
  compression wear.** These need engineered fabric, compression, and flatlock construction —
  you can only get them via **private-label manufacturing** (real MOQs, ~40–60% gross margin).

So the two halves of a gym brand have completely different economics and risk profiles.

## 2. The recommended path: validate cheap, then manufacture the winners

Don't try to launch a full technical range on day one — it's $10k+ and slow. Do this instead:

1. **Phase 1 — POD launch (weeks 1–8):** Launch a small line of **branded printed pieces**
   (oversized gym tee, hoodie, joggers) via POD. Near-zero risk. The goal is NOT big profit —
   it's to **prove your brand/aesthetic resonates and build an audience** while you learn.
2. **Phase 2 — manufacture your winners (month 3+):** Once you know your people respond, invest
   in a **private-label run** of the high-margin technical hero pieces — a **legging + sports
   bra "set"**, which is the backbone product of almost every successful women's gym brand.
3. **Phase 3 — scale the range:** Add colorways and complementary pieces around your proven sets.

This is essentially the **Gymshark playbook**: start small and DTC, nail a niche, build a
community, then expand the technical range as demand proves out.

## 3. Define your niche BEFORE anything else (this is the real work)

"Gym clothes" is not a brand. A brand is **gym wear for someone specific.** Pick a lane:

- Women's lifting / "strong not skinny" aesthetic
- Men's minimalist performance basics
- Running / hybrid athletes
- CrossFit / functional fitness
- Pilates / yoga / low-impact athleisure
- Plus-size athletic wear (underserved, loyal)
- A local gym / community / coach's audience

Your niche decides your fabric, fit, colors, pricing, and — most importantly — **who you make
content for.** A sharp identity people want to *represent* is the entire moat. Everyone can buy
the same blank leggings; they buy *yours* because of what the brand stands for.

## 4. Manufacturing: what to actually know

When you move to private label (Phase 2), here's what matters:

**MOQ (minimum order quantity)**
- Startup-friendly activewear factories run **50–100 units per style/colorway**; some offer
  very low or no-MOQ + free samples. Avoid factories demanding 500+ until you've scaled.

**Fabric specs (don't hand-wave these)**
- **Leggings:** nylon-spandex blend (nylon 6.6), **~280–320 GSM** — this is what makes them
  **opaque / "squat-proof"** and gives compression. Too thin = see-through = returns and refunds.
- Moisture-wicking, four-way stretch, and (increasingly) recycled/sustainable fabrics.

**Construction**
- **Flatlock stitching** is the non-negotiable standard for leggings and sports bras — flat
  seams that prevent chafing. Cheap overlock seams chafe and get returned.

**Tech packs & sampling**
- A **tech pack** (measurements, fabric, colors, stitching, branding) is what you hand the
  factory. Expect **2–4 sample rounds**, 2–6 weeks each, before bulk production.

**Timeline**
- Realistic lead time is **8–16 weeks** from sampling to delivery. Plan cash and launch dates
  around this — it's much slower than dropshipping.

**Vetting a factory:** sportswear specialization, sample quality, communication speed, fabric
sourcing, MOQ flexibility. **Always order samples and physically test them** (wash, stretch,
squat-test) before committing to a bulk run.

## 5. Fit & quality = your survival

In gym wear, **fit is the product.** More returns come from fit/sizing than anything else. Rules:

- Squat-test every legging sample. If it's sheer under stretch, reject the fabric — full stop.
- Publish a **detailed, honest size guide** with real measurements. Vague sizing = return floods.
- Get real people of different body types to wear-test samples before you order in bulk.
- Consistency across restocks matters — customers notice when "the same" legging feels different.

## 6. Pricing

Sell at **at least 4x your landed cost** (product + shipping to you). Activewear carries returns,
size exchanges, and ad costs that eat thin margins. Typical: a set costing you $12–20 to make
retails at $55–90. Don't race to the bottom on price — in gym wear, too-cheap reads as low-quality.

## 7. Marketing: the community playbook (this is how gym brands actually win)

Gym wear is won on **community and content**, not ads-first.

- **Creator/athlete seeding:** send product to micro-influencers and everyday gym-goers in your
  niche. Authentic "in the wild" content beats polished ads. This is how Gymshark started.
- **Short-form video:** film real workouts, fit checks, "get ready with me" gym content. Post
  daily; treat it like a media brand, not a store. Plan 30–50+ videos before judging.
- **Build identity, not just product posts:** the aesthetic, the values, the community name/tag.
  People wear your logo to signal belonging — give them something to belong to.
- **Email/SMS + drops:** limited "drops" create urgency and reward your core fans; email is
  where repeat purchases happen.
- Paid ads come **later**, to amplify the organic content that already proved it converts.

## 8. Realistic budget

| Phase | Spend | What it covers |
|-------|-------|----------------|
| Phase 1 (POD validation) | **$500–1,500** | Designs, Shopify, samples, first content/seeding |
| Phase 2 (first manufactured run) | **$5,000–15,000** | 50–150 units across 1–2 hero styles, tech packs, samples |
| Ongoing (both phases) | budget for it | Photography ($300–800/shoot), branded packaging ($1–3/unit), creator seeding, ads |

> The founders who run out of money aren't the ones who spent too much on product — they're the
> ones who spent **everything** on product and had nothing left for photography, packaging, and
> marketing. Budget as if the garment is only *half* the cost.

## 9. Legal / admin basics

- Register the business + business bank account; set aside for taxes from day one.
- Trademark your brand name/logo before you build equity in it (apparel names get copied fast).
- Honest shipping-time and return/exchange policies — critical in apparel where sizing exchanges
  are constant.
- If you make performance claims ("compression improves recovery"), keep them substantiated and
  modest — advertising claims have to be truthful and backed by evidence.

## 10. First 90 days

- **Weeks 1–2:** Lock your niche + brand identity (name, logo, aesthetic, who it's for).
- **Weeks 2–4:** Design 3–5 printable pieces; set up Shopify + POD; order samples to yourself.
- **Weeks 4–8:** Launch Phase 1. Post daily content, seed to creators, gather sales + fit data.
- **Weeks 8–12:** Identify what resonates → source an activewear manufacturer, request samples of
  your hero set (legging + sports bra), start tech packs. Begin planning your first real drop.

---

**Bottom line:** start with POD to prove the brand cheaply, pour energy into a sharp niche and
community content, then reinvest into manufactured technical sets where the real margin lives.
The garment is the easy part — the brand and the community are the business.
